# Bug fixes, September 2026

What changed, why, how each fix was verified, and what has to be re-run before the
results section of the README can be trusted again.

## 1. Day-0 alignment (`src/erl/events/returns.py`)

**Symptom.** In `04_drift_beats_vs_misses.png` the miss path fell from -0.4% at t=-2 to
-2.0% at t=-1 and only -2.4% at t=0. Three quarters of the reaction sat before "day 0".
The (0, +1) reaction window therefore captured about -0.6% for misses, which is exactly
the Q1 mean in `01_car_by_sue_quintile.png`.

**Cause.** `align_day0` treated `unknown` timing like `amc` and pushed day 0 to the next
trading day. If the earnings endpoint does not supply a `time` field, every event is
`unknown`, so before-open reports were measured one day late and after-close reports
correctly. The mixed sample produced the t=-1 spike with a shoulder at t=0.

**Fix.** `unknown` is now same-day. The (0, +1) window is the standard two-day window
precisely because it covers both a same-day and a next-day reaction when timing is
unknown. `amc` still shifts to the next trading day.

**Guards added.** `build_panel` logs the announcement-time coverage and warns when more
than half the events are `unknown`. `alignment_diagnostic` (in `inference/eventstudy.py`)
computes mean |AR| by relative day from -3 to +3 and warns if the peak is not at 0 or +1.
The table is written to `data/processed/alignment_diagnostic.csv`.

**Tests.** `test_align_day0_unknown_treated_as_same_day`,
`test_unknown_timing_window_covers_before_open_reaction`, and the peak assertion in
`test_build_panel_end_to_end`.

## 2. SUE outliers (`src/erl/events/features.py`)

**Symptom.** `02_surprise_vs_reaction.png` showed SUE at -18 and +26. A standardised
variable should not do that.

**Cause.** SUE = surprise / std of the firm's past surprises. Firms whose past surprises
were all near zero get a near-zero denominator.

**Fix.** `add_sue` winsorises at the 1st/99th percentiles and keeps `sue_raw`. Pass
`winsor=None` to disable. Quintiles are rank-based and unchanged; the continuous-SUE
estimators (OLS slope, double lasso, causal forest) no longer sit on a handful of
leverage points.

**Tests.** `test_sue_is_winsorised_but_raw_is_kept`.

## 3. Degenerate LightGBM (`src/erl/predict/gbm.py`)

**Symptom.** `06_shap_importance.png`: only `sue` had non-zero SHAP, the other eight were
exactly zero (never split on). `05_oos_predicted_vs_actual.png`: predictions spanned
0.1% to 0.5% against actuals of +-17%. The model was a step function of one feature.

**Cause.** Three things compounding:

- The target is in decimals (sd about 0.03). LightGBM's `reg_alpha` (L1 on leaf outputs)
  and `reg_lambda` act on gradient sums on that scale. The Optuna range went up to 10 for
  both, which zeroes every leaf. On a noisy target the near-constant model wins the MSE
  comparison against the unregularised alternatives, so the tuner selected it.
  Verified directly: with sd 0.03 and `reg_alpha=1`, 63 trees are built and half the
  features never split; on percentage points the same settings use all features.
- `num_leaves` up to 127 with `min_child_samples` as low as 10 on roughly 1,400 training
  rows made the unregularised end of the range overfit badly, which pushed the tuner
  further toward the collapsed end.
- `subsample` was tuned but silently ignored: LightGBM only bags when `subsample_freq > 0`.

**Fix.** Fit on `target * 100` (percentage points) and convert predictions and SHAP values
back; `subsample_freq=1`; search space resized for a few thousand events (`num_leaves`
4-31, `max_depth` 2-6, `min_child_samples` 20-100, `reg_alpha` 0-1, `reg_lambda` 0-10,
`learning_rate` 0.01-0.1, `n_estimators` 50-500). `feature_usage` reports splits and gain
per feature; the stage warns when only one feature is used. `gbm_feature_usage.csv` and
`gbm_best_params.csv` are written.

**Tests.** `test_gbm_does_not_collapse_on_decimal_scale_target` (weak signal, decimal
scale, must use at least two features and have non-trivial prediction dispersion).

## 4. Causal-forest confidence intervals (`src/erl/inference/causal_forest.py`)

**Symptom.** `07_forest_moderators.png` showed +-0.0001 error bars around coefficients
of 0.0008 with three of three moderators "significant" on 1,845 events.

**Cause.** The "best linear projection" regressed the forest's fitted CATEs on the
moderators. Fitted CATEs are smooth functions of those same moderators, so the regression
fits them almost perfectly and the standard errors describe the smoothness of the forest,
not sampling uncertainty. On synthetic data with no heterogeneity at all this procedure
gave t-statistics of -30, 5.7, 3.1, 2.4 and -3.9 across five moderators.

**Fix.** Under the partially linear model Y = theta(X) T + g(X, W) + e, Robinson's
residualisation gives Y_res = theta(X) T_res + e, so the BLP of theta(X) on (1, Z) is the
regression Y_res = a T_res + sum_j b_j (T_res x Z_j) + u with cluster-robust (by firm)
errors. The cross-fitted residuals are taken from the fitted `CausalForestDML` (fit with
`cache_values=True`). On the same synthetic null the corrected t-statistics were -1.0,
0.4, 0.5, 0.0 and -0.05. The naive projection is still written to `forest_blp_naive.csv`
so the difference is visible. ATE now comes with a standard error from
`ate_inference` (`forest_ate.csv`).

**Tests.** Extended `test_causal_forest_recovers_heterogeneity` (true moderator t > 3,
null moderator t < 3, naive SE smaller than honest SE) and
`test_blp_standard_errors_are_honest_under_no_heterogeneity` (30 replications: honest BLP
rejects at roughly the nominal rate, naive projection rejects almost always).

## 5. Units and labels (`src/erl/report.py`)

SHAP and BLP figures were on the decimal scale while every other figure was in percent.
Both now plot in percentage points with the unit in the axis label. Figure titles carry n
and the inference method. Em dashes removed from figure text.

# Second pass: audit findings

A line-by-line re-audit after the first pass. Each item below was reproduced
before being fixed; the reproduction is in the test named at the end of the entry.

## 6. Events predating the price history were mapped to the wrong day

`align_day0` called `calendar.searchsorted(date)` and, for a date before the
first trading day in the calendar, got position 0 and returned the first
available trading day. An event from 2015 in a panel whose prices start in 2024
was assigned a day 0 nine years later, and a reaction was computed for it.
Reproduced: `align_day0("2015-03-04", "bmo", <calendar starting 2024-04-29>)`
returned `2024-04-29`. Now returns `None`.
Test: `test_align_day0_before_price_history_returns_none`.

## 7. Unadjusted prices turn splits into fake reactions

`harvest/prices.py` uses `/stable/historical-price-eod/full` and falls back to
`close` when `adjClose` is absent, so on a plan that does not return `adjClose`
the whole panel is unadjusted. A 4:1 split then reads as a -75% one-day return,
and if it lands in an event window it becomes a fabricated earnings reaction.
Reproduced: a synthetic 4:1 split produced `ret = -0.75`. Added
`suspected_split_artifacts` (gross ratio within 1.5% of a whole-number split
ratio, index symbols excluded) and a warning at `daily_returns` time, plus a
warning for returns that span a price gap of more than a week (those are
multi-day returns wearing a daily label).
Test: `test_split_artifacts_are_flagged`.

## 8. The drift path mixed event cohorts

`ar_path` kept every event on every day for which a return existed, so events
dropped out as the window extended and the cumulative line was a sum of means
taken over different samples. A level shift at day 12 could be composition
rather than drift. Reproduced with two events where one has a short price
history: n fell from 2 to 1 at day 3 while `cum_ar` carried on. Now
`require_full_window=True` by default (one fixed cohort across -5 to +25) with
a warning if the per-day counts still vary by more than 5%.

## 9. Out-of-sample R-squared used the test fold's own mean

`regression_metrics` benchmarked against `y_true.mean()` of the test fold, which
is information the model did not have. On a test period whose mean differs from
the training period this penalises a model that is doing nothing wrong.
Reproduced: a model that predicts the training mean scored r2 = -0.41 rather
than 0. `r2` now benchmarks against the training mean, `r2_within` keeps the old
number, and `r2_benchmark` records which was used. This changes every reported
R-squared, including the "negative out-of-sample R-squared for all three models"
claim in the README.
Test: `test_oos_r2_uses_the_training_mean_as_benchmark`.

## 10. Winsorisation leaked future data into the out-of-time evaluation

The first pass winsorised SUE on full-sample quantiles. For the inference track
that is fine (it is explicitly a full-sample estimate), but the prediction track
is evaluated out of time and a clipping threshold computed from 2024 data is
still 2024 data. Reproduced: the 1st/99th cut moved from -2.71/+2.65 (full
sample) to -2.50/+2.65 (first half only). `add_sue` now defaults to
`winsor_mode="asof"`, computing quantiles from strictly prior events, with
`"pooled"` still available. Events before 100 prior observations exist are left
unclipped, and that count is logged; if the whole sample is smaller than
`min_prior` the function warns that it did nothing.
Test: `test_asof_winsorisation_uses_no_future_data`.

## 11. The two momentum horizons used different return conventions

`runup_20d` and `runup_60d` were market-adjusted (abnormal) returns while
`momentum_12_1` was a raw total return. The README's headline interpretation,
"short-term run-up dampens the reaction while long-term momentum amplifies it,
two horizons pulling in opposite directions", was therefore comparing an
abnormal-return feature with a raw-return one, so part of the contrast was
market beta rather than the firm. `momentum_12_1` is now the market-adjusted
12-1 return, matching the run-ups, and `momentum_12_1_raw` keeps the total-return
version for reference. This changes the moderator result.
Test: `test_price_features_use_one_return_convention`.

## 12. Fundamentals were attached before they were published

`merge_fundamentals` matched each event to the latest key-metrics row whose
`date` was on or before the announcement, but that `date` is the fiscal period
end, not the filing date. A period ending 31 March is typically published 60-90
days later, so an event in April or May was handed figures from a report that
did not yet exist: look-ahead bias in `pe_z`, `pe_ratio` and `mcap_decile`.
Fundamental dates are now shifted forward by `publication_lag_days=90` before
the as-of match. The existing end-to-end test had encoded the old behaviour and
was corrected.
Test: `test_fundamentals_are_not_used_before_publication`.

## 13. Structural breaks were never tested

Every estimate pooled 2015-2024: a zero-rate regime, the COVID volatility
shock, the 2022 rate repricing. A pooled coefficient across a break is an
average of two different economies. Added `inference/stability.py`:

- `regime_stability`: the surprise effect by regime (breaks at 2020-03-01 and
  2022-01-01, chosen from the macro record rather than from the outcome), with
  regime fixed effects, and a Wald test that the regime interactions are jointly
  zero. Two-way (firm x quarter) clustered, so it is a Chow test that does not
  assume homoskedasticity or independence.
- `rolling_effect`: the slope on a rolling 400-event window, to distinguish a
  discrete break from a drift.

Both are written to CSV by the inference stage
(`regime_stability.csv`, `regime_stability_test.csv`, `rolling_effect.csv`) and
plotted as `09_effect_stability.png`. Verified against synthetic data with an
injected break (detected, p < 0.01) and without one (not detected, p = 0.41).
Tests: `test_regime_stability_detects_a_real_break`,
`test_regime_stability_does_not_invent_a_break`, `test_rolling_effect_tracks_the_slope`.

## 14. Sample attrition was invisible

`dropna` over all features silently removed 13-16% of the panel and pushed the
effective start date roughly a year past the panel start, because `momentum_12_1`
needs 252 prior trading days and SUE needs four prior quarters. The prediction
stage now logs rows kept, rows dropped, and effective versus panel start date,
and warns above 10%. The behaviour is unchanged on purpose: the linear baselines
cannot take NaNs, so dropping keeps the ML-versus-linear comparison
like-for-like. Worth stating in the README rather than silently fixing.

## Not changed, but you should know

- **Market-adjusted, not beta-adjusted.** Abnormal return is `ret - bench_ret`,
  i.e. beta is assumed to be 1 for every firm. For a high-beta name on a day the
  index moves 2%, a meaningful part of the "abnormal" return is beta. A market
  model estimated over a pre-event window is the standard alternative.
- **No volatility scaling.** A 3% reaction for a utility and for a high-beta
  software name are treated as the same magnitude. Standardising by pre-event
  idiosyncratic volatility is common and would change the quintile means.
- **`mcap_decile` is relative to the pilot sample.** It is a within-quarter
  decile over the 84 names in the panel, not over the market, so "decile 10"
  means large within this sample. Fine as a control, misleading if described as
  a size decile.
- **`embargo_days` in `PurgedWalkForwardCV` does nothing.** Training is always
  strictly before the purge cutoff, so an embargo after the test window can
  never bind in an expanding-window design. Verified: `embargo_days=0` and
  `embargo_days=365` give identical fold sizes. The purge is what protects
  against the 21-day drift outcome overlapping the test window, and 35 calendar
  days is roughly 24 trading days, which covers it. Harmless but the parameter
  advertises a protection it is not providing.
- **Transcript linking uses `direction="nearest"`.** Within a 7-day tolerance a
  call can be matched to an event after it as easily as before it. Calls happen
  at or just after the announcement so there is no obvious leakage into the day
  0-1 reaction, but a signed rule would be safer.
- **`usable_features` computes coverage on the full panel**, so the feature set
  is chosen with full-sample information. The effect is small and it is not
  really tunable, but it is not strictly point-in-time either.

# Sample-span note (Premium tier)

With a data plan that reaches further back, `ERL_START_DATE` can move earlier
than the 2015 default. Two constraints decide how far:

1. **Point-in-time index membership.** `universe.py` falls back to current S&P
   500 members (or an 84-name subset) with a warning when
   `/stable/historical-sp500-constituent` is unavailable. Extending the start
   date under that fallback makes survivorship bias worse rather than better:
   the sample would run today's index back through 2008 with the firms that
   failed simply absent. Confirm the historical endpoint returns dated
   add/remove records before extending.
2. **Analyst estimate coverage.** `surprise = eps_actual - eps_estimate`, so
   estimate history, not price history, is the real limit. Check how far back
   `epsEstimated` is non-null on the earnings endpoint; the earliest usable
   analysis start is that date plus roughly 18 months, since SUE needs four
   prior quarters and `momentum_12_1` needs 252 trading days.

2005-01-01 is a defensible floor even with 30 years available. Regulation FD
(October 2000), decimalization (2001) and the 2003 Global Research Analyst
Settlement each changed how earnings information reaches the market, so a panel
crossing them pools institutional regimes and not just macroeconomic ones.

`DEFAULT_BREAKS` in `inference/stability.py` now covers the GFC, its aftermath,
the COVID shock and the 2022 repricing. `applicable_breaks` drops any break that
falls outside the sample or that would leave fewer than 150 events on either
side, so the same list works for a 2005 start and a 2015 one without producing
an empty regime and a singular design matrix. Verified in
`test_breaks_outside_the_sample_are_dropped`,
`test_breaks_are_kept_when_the_sample_reaches_back`,
`test_narrow_window_is_dropped_in_a_thin_sample`.

# Third pass: point-in-time membership (after confirming the historical endpoint works)

The change log from `/stable/historical-sp500-constituent` is a joiner/leaver
feed, not a snapshot, so membership is reconstructed by rolling the log back
from the current constituent list. Two problems in that reconstruction.

## 15. Removal-only records were read as additions

`fetch_membership` took `added = record["symbol"]` unconditionally. In the
payload `symbol` is the ticker that joined and `removedTicker` the one that
left, but a removal-only row can echo the departing ticker in `symbol` with
`addedSecurity` blank. Reading `symbol` as the joiner then does two wrong things
at once: the backward pass discards and re-adds the same ticker (a no-op, so the
firm never gets restored to the start-date membership set) and the forward pass
opens a fresh spell beginning on the very day the firm left the index. A firm
delisted in 2009 would appear as having *joined* in 2009. `parse_change` now
treats `symbol` as an addition only when `addedSecurity` names something or the
record carries no removal at all.
Test: `test_removal_only_record_is_not_read_as_an_addition`.

Also removed a `dateAdded` fallback in `_parse_date`. Only `date` is the change
date; `dateAdded` is when the *added* security joined, which on a removal row is
an unrelated and much earlier date, so the fallback could place a 2009 removal
in 1957. Unmatched removals (a removal with no open spell) are now counted and
warned about, since they indicate the change log is incomplete before the start
date.

## 16. Correct membership does not by itself remove survivorship bias

Reconstructing the universe is half the job. If the price endpoint returns
nothing for a firm that was delisted, acquired or taken private, that firm
disappears at the panel stage and the bias returns, now harder to detect because
the universe step logged success. `membership_coverage` compares the
reconstructed universe against the tickers actually present in
`prices.parquet`, split by whether the name left the index during the sample,
and warns when coverage of departed names trails current members by more than 10
percentage points. Written to `universe_coverage.csv` and `universe_missing.csv`
by the harvest stage.

This number is the evidence for or against describing the panel as
survivorship-bias-free. Report it rather than asserting the property. Note also
that ticker changes (FB to META) appear in the log as a removal plus an
addition, and price history under the retired ticker may not exist, which shows
up in the same diagnostic.
Tests: `test_membership_closes_the_spell_of_a_delisted_name`,
`test_coverage_flags_missing_delisted_names`.

# Fourth pass: what the earnings payload actually contains

Two findings from inspecting a real `/stable/earnings` response (JNJ, back to 2002).

## 17. Placeholder estimates produce spurious zero surprises

Where no consensus existed, the feed fills the estimate field with the reported
figure. A 2002 JNJ row returns `epsActual 0.39 / epsEstimated 0.39` together
with `revenueActual 8403000000 / revenueEstimated 8403000000`: identical to the
dollar. `surprise = epsActual - epsEstimated` is then exactly zero for reasons
that have nothing to do with the market being unsurprised. These rows cluster in
the early years, so extending the start date would load the earliest regime with
artificial zero-surprise events and corrupt precisely the cross-period
comparison that `stability.py` exists to make.

Revenue is the discriminating test. A real consensus revenue estimate is a
ten-digit number and will not match the reported figure to the dollar; the same
JNJ series shows `revenueEstimated 8594813559` against `revenueActual
8743000000` one quarter later, which is what a genuine estimate looks like. An
exact EPS match is *not* evidence on its own, because analysts genuinely hit a
cents-rounded EPS fairly often, so it only counts where revenue is unavailable
to check.

`parse_calendar` now sets `estimate_backfilled`, the harvest logs the rate by
year and writes `estimate_backfill_by_year.csv`, and `build_panel` drops flagged
events with a warning. Read that CSV before setting `ERL_START_DATE`: the first
year where the placeholder share is low is the first year in which a surprise
variable means anything.
Tests: `test_placeholder_estimates_are_flagged_not_trusted`,
`test_backfill_rate_by_year_reports_the_early_cluster`.

## 18. The payload has no announcement-time field

The stable earnings response carries `symbol`, `date`, `epsActual`,
`epsEstimated`, `revenueActual`, `revenueEstimated` and `lastUpdated`. There is
no `time` field, so `_normalize_time` returns `"unknown"` for every event and
the panel's timing-coverage log will report 100% unknown. That is the case the
day-0 fix in item 1 was built for: day 0 is the announcement date and the
two-day (0, +1) window absorbs before-open and after-close reporting. It is not
a bug, but it has to be stated in the README's Data section, because it means
the reaction window cannot be tightened to a single day.
Test: `test_missing_time_field_becomes_unknown`.

Two related notes: `fiscalDateEnding` is also absent, so `fiscal_date_ending`
falls back to the announcement date and should not be read as a period end. And
`lastUpdated: 2025-04-24` means the estimates are a current-database snapshot
rather than the consensus as it stood before each announcement, so restatements
and backfills cannot be ruled out. That is a disclosure item, not something the
code can fix.

# Fifth pass: gated index symbols

## 19. ^TNX returns 402 and silently emptied a feature

On the live plan `/stable/historical-price-eod/full` rejects `^TNX` with a 402
("not available under your current subscription"). The harvest logged one
failure and carried on, which is correct behaviour, but `add_market_state` then
looked up a symbol that was not in the price data and `rate_level` came out
entirely NaN for every event. The feature-coverage filter drops it, so the
pipeline completes and the interest-rate control simply vanishes from the
analysis without anything in the results saying so.

`vix_symbol` and `rate_symbol` are now ordered fallback chains
(`^VIX, VIXY, VXX` and `^TNX, ^TYX, IEF, TLT`). `_first_available` picks the
first symbol whose prices actually arrived, warns when it is not the preferred
one, and records the choice in the panel's attrs and in
`market_state_symbols.csv`. The proxies are harvested by default via
`extra_benchmarks`.

An ETF proxy is not the same quantity as the index: IEF is a bond-fund price
level, so higher IEF means *lower* yields, the opposite sign to `^TNX`. Any
interpretation of the rate control has to account for that, which is why the
symbol actually used is written out rather than left implicit.
Tests: `test_gated_index_symbol_falls_back_to_a_proxy`,
`test_rate_feature_is_populated_from_the_proxy`.


## 20. DataFrame.attrs did not survive the fundamentals merge

`add_market_state` recorded which market-state symbol was actually used
(item 19) in `panel.attrs`, but `merge_fundamentals` runs a merge and pandas
drops `attrs` across merges, so by the time `build_panel` copied them onto the
returned frame there was nothing left and `market_state_symbols.csv` was never
written. Observed on the live pilot: the proxy-substitution warning fired for
`^TNX -> IEF`, yet no CSV appeared.

The symbols are now captured into a local dict immediately after
`add_market_state` and applied to the result at the end.

The original test passed while the bug was live because it called `build_panel`
without fundamentals, which short-circuits the merge and preserves `attrs`. It
now supplies a fundamentals frame, and was checked to fail against the old code
before being accepted.

# Sixth pass: the model comparison had no test behind it

## 21. "Statistically indistinguishable" was an assertion, not a result

The prediction stage reported R-squared, MAE and rank-IC per model and the
README concluded that LightGBM and OLS were statistically indistinguishable out
of sample. No test existed anywhere in the codebase. On the corrected data the
raw metrics now mildly favour LightGBM (rank-IC 0.171 against 0.148 for OLS),
so the claim needs evidence whichever way it goes.

The ordering of those metrics carries almost no information on a single test
fold of a few hundred events: the standard error of a Spearman correlation at
n = 400 is roughly 0.05, so a rank-IC gap of 0.02 is noise. And because the
models predict the same events from the same features, their errors are highly
correlated, so the comparison has to be paired.

`predict/compare.py` adds two paired tests:

- `loss_differential_test`: the Diebold-Mariano idea for a panel. The per-event
  squared-error (or absolute-error) difference is regressed on a constant with
  errors clustered on calendar quarter, since events in the same quarter share
  market-wide shocks. The constant is the mean loss advantage and its
  t-statistic is the test.
- `rank_ic_difference_test`: a block bootstrap of the rank-IC gap that resamples
  whole quarters rather than individual events, preserving within-quarter
  cross-sectional dependence. Resampling events independently would understate
  the standard error for the same reason the naive causal-forest projection did
  (item 4).

`compare_models` runs both for each challenger against LightGBM and labels each
pair "differs" or "indistinguishable", written to
`prediction_significance.csv`. The baseline stage now carries `event_id` so the
two prediction frames are joined on identity rather than row position.

Validated in both directions: two near-identical models are not separated, and a
model with genuine signal against one predicting noise is separated with
`mse_pvalue < 0.05` and the expected signs.
Tests: `test_paired_tests_do_not_separate_equally_good_models`,
`test_paired_tests_detect_a_genuinely_better_model`,
`test_block_bootstrap_respects_quarterly_clustering`.

# Seventh pass: is a regime break a pricing change or a volatility artifact?

## 22. Reactions were never volatility-standardised

The live pilot rejects parameter stability (Wald chi2(3) = 9.21, p = 0.027) with
the post-2022 regime at 0.0096 against 0.0033 for 2009-2020, and the rolling
series ranges from 0.0007 to 0.0094 across the sample. Before any of that can
be reported as a change in how the market prices earnings news, one alternative
has to be ruled out.

SUE is standardised by construction; the abnormal return is not. In a
high-volatility period the same informational surprise produces a mechanically
larger abnormal return, so the regression coefficient rises with no change in
price formation at all. The periods where the estimated effect is largest are
exactly the high-volatility ones (2022 onward, and 2011-2013), so the headline
finding and the leading artifact predict the same pattern.

Added:

- `add_idiosyncratic_vol`: standard deviation of daily abnormal returns over
  (-60, -11) trading days. The window ends eleven days before day 0 so neither
  the reaction nor the run-up into it can inflate the denominator that is meant
  to scale the reaction.
- `add_vol_adjusted_target`: `car_reaction_vol_adj = car_reaction / idio_vol`,
  with the denominator floored at its 1st percentile so a few unusually quiet
  stocks cannot generate enormous standardised reactions, which would reproduce
  the SUE-denominator problem (item 2) one column over.

The inference stage now runs `regime_stability` and `rolling_effect` on both
targets, writing `regime_stability_voladj.csv`,
`regime_stability_voladj_test.csv` and `rolling_effect_voladj.csv`. If the break
survives volatility standardisation it is a pricing change; if it disappears, it
was scaling, and that is the result to report.

Validated on synthetic data where volatility triples between two periods and the
pricing relationship is held fixed: the raw coefficient more than doubles while
the vol-adjusted one stays within 0.7 to 1.4 of its first-period value.
Tests: `test_idiosyncratic_vol_excludes_the_event_window`,
`test_vol_adjusted_target_floors_the_denominator`,
`test_vol_adjustment_removes_a_pure_volatility_shift`.

# Eighth pass: secret leakage and where the survivorship gap sits

## 23. The API key was written into every log line

The FMP key travels as a query parameter, and httpx logs the full request URL at
INFO level, so `python -m erl.pipeline harvest > harvest.txt` produced a file
with the key repeated on every line. `.gitignore` covered `.env` but had no
pattern for log files, so the obvious next step (commit the run output) would
have published the key.

The httpx and httpcore loggers are now capped at WARNING in `main()`, and
`.gitignore` covers `*.log`, `*_log.txt` and `harvest_*.txt`. Any log file
produced before this change should be deleted rather than committed, and a key
that appeared in one should be rotated.

## 24. A single coverage number hides which period the bias falls on

The full-universe harvest reports 100% coverage of current constituents and
76.3% of the 465 names that left the index, a 24 percentage point gap over 110
firms. That headline figure says nothing about *where* the gap is, and it is
not spread evenly: firms that left long ago are less likely to be retrievable,
so the missing names concentrate in the early sample. That matters because the
early sample is one side of every comparison the stability analysis makes, so
residual survivorship bias falls hardest on exactly the regimes being contrasted.

`coverage_by_era` breaks coverage of departed names down by removal year,
written to `universe_coverage_by_era.csv` and logged. Report it alongside the
headline number.
Tests: `test_coverage_by_era_locates_the_survivorship_gap`.

# Ninth pass: a readable results export

## 25. Results were scattered across two dozen CSVs

The pipeline writes each stage's output to its own CSV, which is right for
reproducibility and unhelpful for reading. `export.py` adds a final `export`
stage that produces three artifacts in the processed directory:

- `results.xlsx` — one sheet per result table, with a **Summary** sheet first
  that pulls out the headline numbers. Arial throughout, bold frozen header row,
  auto-fitted columns. Values only, never formulas: these are computed research
  outputs, not a model meant to recalculate.
- `results.md` — the same content as markdown, for reading in a terminal or
  pasting into a write-up. Tables over 60 rows are truncated to the first 30 and
  last 10, with a pointer to the CSV.
- `results_summary.csv` — just the headline table.

The Summary sheet is the part worth the effort. Every row carries the source
file it was read from, so any figure traces back to the stage that produced it,
and several rows carry the interpretive caveat that belongs with the number:
that the survivorship coverage figure means the panel is *not* bias-free, that
the naive BLP standard errors are shown only for contrast and are not a valid
inference, that rolling windows overlap and are therefore not independent
observations, and that GBM gain shares are in-sample and say nothing about
out-of-sample skill. It also computes a few things a reader would otherwise have
to do by hand: the |AR| peak day and its ratio to the pre-event baseline, the
largest standard-error understatement between the honest and naive projections,
which moderators survive at 5% under each, and the ratio between the largest and
smallest regime effects.

Nothing in this module computes a result. Every figure is read back from a CSV
an earlier stage wrote.

`openpyxl` and `tabulate` added to requirements. Run it with
`python -m erl.pipeline export`, or as the last step of
`python -m erl.pipeline all`.
Tests: seven in `tests/test_export.py`, covering sheet ordering, the extracted
figures, Excel sheet-name limits (31 characters, invalid characters, collisions),
long-table truncation, an empty directory, and that all three artifacts land.


## 26. The export could present two different runs as one set of results

The processed directory is just a folder. A full-universe harvest followed by an
analysis that was never re-run leaves data-quality figures describing 54,158
events (465 departed tickers, 1,892 placeholder estimates) sitting in the same
summary table as results estimated on 2,115 events from a 31-ticker pilot. Every
individual number is correct and the table as a whole is misleading, which is
worse than a wrong number because nothing looks off.

Observed on the live run: the first exported summary mixed exactly that.

The harvest and panel stages now write `harvest_manifest.csv` and
`panel_manifest.csv` recording universe, ticker and event counts and the date
range. `provenance_check` compares them and the Summary sheet opens with the
verdict: `consistent`, `MISMATCH` with the two ticker counts and instructions to
re-run, or `unknown` when manifests are absent. A second row states the analysis
sample explicitly, so a reader always knows what n the results below rest on.
Tests: `test_provenance_flags_a_mixed_run`,
`test_provenance_passes_on_a_single_run`,
`test_provenance_is_unknown_without_manifests`.

# Tenth pass: prices were never split-adjusted

## 27. Neither FMP price endpoint adjusts for splits

The split screen added in item 7 fired on the full universe: 41 split-shaped
returns, the example being AGN at -49.3% on 2007-06-25. The obvious fix was to
switch `PRICE_ENDPOINT` to `historical-price-eod/dividend-adjusted`. Tested
against that exact date first, and it does not work:

    2007-06-22  adjClose 114.47
    2007-06-25  adjClose  58.00

The "dividend-adjusted" endpoint adjusts for dividends and **not** for splits.
Switching endpoints would have changed nothing while creating the impression the
problem was fixed.

Why it mattered more than 41 events suggests:

- a split inside a (0, +1) reaction window fabricates a +-50% earnings reaction;
- inside a 20, 60 or 252-day run-up window it corrupts the feature;
- inside the 50-day window behind `idio_vol` (item 22) it corrupts the
  denominator that scales every reaction for that firm across a two-month
  stretch. With the vol-adjusted regime test as the project's headline result,
  41 artifacts could compromise on the order of 2,000 volatility denominators,
  and splits and reverse splits cluster in exactly the crisis periods the
  stability analysis compares.

`harvest/splits.py` fetches `/stable/splits` per ticker and applies the
adjustment: a split with numerator n and denominator d multiplies every price
strictly before the split date by d/n, compounding across multiple splits, so
prices from the split date onward are untouched. Reverse splits (n < d) scale
prices up and are handled by the same arithmetic. Splits before the sample start
are skipped, since those prices are already on the post-split basis.

The harvest applies this before writing `prices.parquet`, then re-runs the
detection screen on the adjusted series and writes the residual count to
`split_adjustment.csv`, which the export surfaces. Zero is the target; a
remainder means the splits feed missed events and those returns are still
fabricated. When no split data is available at all the prices are left
untouched with a warning rather than silently guessed at from price jumps,
because a genuine -50% day would otherwise be "corrected" into nonexistence.

Tested against the real AGN series as FMP returns it: the raw series produces a
return below -45%, and after adjustment no daily move exceeds 5%, the
2007-06-22 close becomes 57.235 (half of 114.47), the post-split closes are
unchanged, and `verify_adjustment` returns 0. Also covered: compounding across
two splits, reverse splits, unaffected tickers left byte-identical, and the
empty-feed path.
Tests: eight in `tests/test_splits.py`.


## 28. Splits were fetched for the equity universe but not the price frame

`prices.parquet` holds the equity universe *plus* the benchmarks and ETF
proxies, and the first version of item 27 requested splits only for the equity
tickers. The proxies split too: VIXY has reverse-split repeatedly, and an
unadjusted 1-for-4 reverse split reads as a +300% one-day move, which would
corrupt `vix_level` as a control and (if VIXY were ever selected by the fallback
chain in item 19) the volatility control for every event around it.

Split requests now cover the price frame, de-duplicated, excluding `^`-prefixed
index symbols, which never split and are not tradable.

Also in this pass: an empty price harvest now logs an error rather than silently
leaving a stale `prices.parquet` for the downstream stages to run on, and
`verify_adjustment` no longer logs the detection warning a second time when it
re-screens the adjusted series.

Edge cases verified by hand: a split date absent from the price series (a halt)
still adjusts the prices before it; 3-for-2 and other fractional ratios work;
duplicate feed rows for one date are de-duplicated so the factor is not applied
twice; row counts and unaffected tickers are preserved exactly; NaN prices stay
NaN; malformed feed rows (missing ratio, unparseable date) are dropped while
string-typed numerators are coerced. `volume` is left on its as-traded basis:
nothing in the project computes anything from it, and rescaling it would make
the stored figure disagree with external sources for no benefit.
Tests: `test_etf_proxies_are_in_the_split_symbol_list`,
`test_reverse_split_on_a_proxy_is_corrected`.


## 29. The feed back-adjusts some symbols, so blind adjustment made things worse

Applying item 27 to the full universe adjusted 618 split events across 313
tickers and left **193** split-shaped returns behind, up from 41 before the
adjustment. The correction was creating artifacts, not removing them.

The cause is that FMP back-adjusts inconsistently. AGN, delisted in 2020,
returns a raw traded price across its 2007 split, which is what the original
test was built on. Other symbols, apparently the still-active ones, come back
already adjusted. Applying the factor to a series that is already continuous
introduces a jump of the same magnitude in the opposite direction, so the more
splits the feed reported, the more damage the "fix" did.

`split_is_present` now checks each split event against the prices before the
factor is applied: it compares the observed gross return across the split date
with the value expected from the ratio (for a 2:1 split, an unadjusted series
falls by half, so observed x ratio is about 1). Three outcomes, all counted and
reported in `split_adjustment.csv` as `splits_applied`,
`splits_already_adjusted` and `splits_ambiguous`:

- the jump is visible, so the adjustment is applied;
- the series is already continuous across the date, so the vendor adjusted it
  and nothing is done;
- neither (a genuine large move happening to coincide with a split date), so it
  is left alone rather than "corrected" on a guess.

The tolerance is 15%, loose enough to absorb ordinary price movement on the
split day and tight enough to separate "halved" from "continuous".

The general lesson is worth recording: a vendor field that is correct for the
symbol you tested is not necessarily correct for the other 700. The
verification step from item 27 is what surfaced this, and it is the reason the
count is reported rather than assumed.
Tests: `test_already_adjusted_series_is_not_adjusted_again`,
`test_visible_split_is_still_detected_and_applied`,
`test_mixed_feed_adjusts_only_the_unadjusted_ticker`,
`test_genuine_large_move_on_a_split_date_is_left_alone`.

## 30. What the residual artifacts actually are

After item 29 the live run reports 618 feed splits: 85 visible in the prices and
applied, 396 already adjusted by the vendor, 137 ambiguous, and 38 split-shaped
returns remaining. Inspecting the 38 by hand shows they are three different
things, and conflating them would have led to the wrong action:

- **Genuine price moves.** FRC -49% on 2023-04-25 is First Republic's collapse;
  CIT -75% in July 2009 the near-bankruptcy; KODK +203% on 2020-07-28 the DFC
  loan announcement; HIG, DAN and CAR at around +100% in March 2009 are
  GFC-era financials. These are real and must not be "corrected". They are the
  screen's false-positive rate, and that rate is the price of a screen loose
  enough to catch real splits.
- **Real splits the feed does not cover.** AGN's 2007 2:1 split is in this list
  even though it is the event the whole adjustment was built from, because the
  feed does not return it. UA on 2016-04-08 was a 2-for-1 Class C distribution.
  Coverage of delisted names and share-class events is patchy.
- **A recycled ticker.** CPWR accounts for 15 of the 38 on its own, with 2:1,
  3:1, 5:1 and 10:1 moves inside a few months of 2025. Compuware was delisted
  in 2014, so this is one symbol holding two companies' price history spliced
  together. Every return across the join is meaningless, and so is every
  feature built from them.

Three additions, all reporting rather than silently filtering:

- `unexplained_artifacts` separates "the feed covers this ticker but reports no
  split near this date" from "the feed does not cover this ticker at all". A
  ticker-level merge conflates them and makes the second case vanish, which is
  how the first diagnostic pass wrongly concluded that none of the 38 were near
  a split.
- `suspicious_tickers` flags symbols with three or more unexplained
  split-shaped moves, the signature of a recycled ticker. Flagged, not dropped:
  excluding a name from the sample is a decision for the write-up, not a silent
  filter.
- `events_touched` counts events with a residual artifact anywhere inside a
  feature window. The reaction window is two days, but `idio_vol` reaches back
  60 trading days and the run-ups 20 and 60, so one bad return contaminates far
  more than one event. Written to `artifact_exposure.csv` with a warning above
  2%.

Tests: `test_unexplained_distinguishes_uncovered_tickers_from_covered_ones`,
`test_recycled_ticker_is_flagged_but_a_single_crash_is_not`,
`test_events_touched_counts_the_volatility_window_not_just_the_reaction`.
